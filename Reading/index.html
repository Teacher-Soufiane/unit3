UNIT: 3EDUCATIONREADING COMPREHENSION
1Literacy is often described as the light that helps individuals and societies find their way toward progress. Being able to read, write, and understand information opens doors to better jobs, informed decision-making, and active citizenship. However, literacy is not only acquired inside classrooms; it can be gained through three different paths: formal, non-formal, and informal education. Understanding the differences between these three approaches shows just how many opportunities exist for people to become literate at any stage of their lives.
2Formal education is the most structured of the three. It usually takes place in schools, colleges, and universities, following a fixed curriculum, timetable, and set of examinations. Students who complete formal education receive official certificates or diplomas that are recognized by employers and institutions. Because it is carefully planned and monitored by the government or educational authorities, formal education tends to build strong, systematic literacy skills over many years.
3Non-formal education, on the other hand, is organized but takes place outside the traditional school system. It includes adult literacy programs, vocational training courses, and community workshops designed for people who missed the chance to attend formal school. These programs are usually more flexible, with shorter schedules and practical goals, such as teaching a farmer to read agricultural instructions or helping a mother learn to write a simple letter. Non-formal education plays a crucial role in reducing illiteracy among adults and out-of-school youth.
4Informal education is the least structured type, yet it may be the most constant throughout a person's life. It happens naturally through daily experiences: conversations with family members, observing others, watching television, or exploring the internet. Unlike formal and non-formal education, informal learning has no fixed curriculum, no classroom, and no certificate at the end. Nevertheless, it plays an essential role in shaping vocabulary, general knowledge, and communication skills from early childhood onward.
5Rather than competing with one another, these three types of education actually complement each other. A child may begin developing early language skills informally at home, continue building literacy formally at school, and later strengthen practical skills through non-formal training as an adult. Together, they ensure that literacy remains accessible to everyone, regardless of age or circumstance. In this way, literacy truly lights the way — not through a single path, but through many roads that all lead toward the same destination: a more informed and capable society.
